Going in circles: circular motion and gravitation

1. Moving in a circle

Uniform circular motion means moving in a circle at a constant speed. The speed stays the same, but the direction of the velocity changes every instant, and a change in velocity is an acceleration. That acceleration always points toward the center of the circle.

Tangential velocity

Points along the tangent to the circle. One lap covers 2πr in one period T.

v = 2πrT

Centripetal acceleration

Points toward the center. Faster or tighter circles mean more acceleration.

ac = v²r

Centripetal force

The net force toward the center. It points the same way as ac.

Fc = mac = mv²r

Centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It’s a job title for whichever real forces point toward the center: tension, friction, gravity, or the normal force. Never draw an extra arrow labeled “Fc” on a free-body diagram.

3. The method

This is the same T-chart from the Newton’s laws lessons. The only change: in the column that points toward the center, the right side is mv²r instead of ma.

  1. Find the circle. Where is the center, and what is r? The center is in the plane of the circle, which isn’t always where the string is tied.
  2. Draw a free-body diagram with real forces only. Draw ac as a separate arrow beside it, pointing toward the center.
  3. Choose axes: c toward the center, and y perpendicular to the circle (usually vertical).
  4. Draw the T-chart. Start with ΣFc = mv²r and ΣFy = 0.
  5. Assign signs. In the c-column, forces pointing toward the center are + and forces pointing away are −.
  6. Solve the column with only one unknown first, box the answer, and carry it across.
  7. Check: does it make sense? A track pushes harder at the bottom of a dip and less at the top of a hill.

4. Horizontal circles

When something circles in a horizontal plane, the c-column is horizontal and the y-column is vertical. With nothing moving up or down, ΣFy = 0.

    5. Vertical circles and apparent weight

    A bathroom scale doesn’t measure your weight; it measures how hard it pushes up on you. That push, the normal force, is your apparent weight. In a vertical circle, the c-direction is up or down, and only one column is needed.

    Bottom of a dip or loop

    Center is above you. The track pushes harder than gravity: you feel heavier.

    FN − Fg = mv²r

    Top of a hill

    Center is below you. The road pushes less than gravity: you feel lighter.

    Fg − FN = mv²r

    Top of a loop (inside)

    Center is below you, and the track pushes down on you, toward the center.

    FN + Fg = mv²r

    When the normal force reaches zero, gravity alone supplies the centripetal force: mg = mv²r, so v = √gr. That’s the fastest you can go over a hill without leaving the road, and the slowest you can go through the top of a loop without falling.

    Worked example: the top of a loop

    A 60 kg rider goes through the top of a vertical loop of radius 10 m at 15 m/s. Find the normal force from the track on the rider (their apparent weight), and the slowest speed at which they could pass the top.

    Top at 15 m/s
    Slowest (FN = 0)

    ΣFc = mv²r

    FN + Fg = mv²r

    FN = mv²r − mg

    FN = 60(15)²10 − 600

    FN = 750 N

    ΣFc = mv²r

    0 + mg = mv²r

    v = √gr = √10(10)

    vmin = 10 m/s

      6. Conical pendulums

      A mass on a string swings in a horizontal circle while the string sweeps out a cone. Two things trip people up:

      • The center of the circle is level with the mass, directly below the pivot, not at the pivot. The radius is r = L sin θ.
      • Tension does two jobs. Its vertical part, T cos θ, holds up the weight. Its horizontal part, T sin θ, points toward the center and supplies the centripetal force. (θ is measured from the vertical.)

      Worked example

      A 2 kg ball hangs from a 2.0 m string and moves in a horizontal circle with the string at 37° from the vertical. Find the tension and the ball’s speed.

      Find the circle first

      y

      ΣFc = mv²r

      Tc = mv²r

      T sin θ = mv²r

      25(0.6) = 2v²1.2T carried from the y-column

      v² = 9

      v = 3 m/s

      ΣFy = 0

      Ty − Fg = 0

      T cos θ − Fg = 0

        7. Gravity: a fundamental force and its field

        Physicists know of four fundamental interactions: gravitational, electromagnetic, strong nuclear, and weak nuclear. Gravity is by far the weakest, yet it shapes planets, stars, and galaxies. It always attracts, it reaches across any distance, and mass never cancels the way positive and negative charges do.

        Newton’s law of gravitation

        Every pair of masses attracts each other along the line between their centers.

        Fg = Gm1m2r²

        r is the center-to-center distance. By Newton’s third law, Earth pulls you exactly as hard as you pull Earth.

        Gravitational field

        A mass M creates a field in the space around it. The field is the force per kilogram on anything placed there.

        g = Fgm = GMr²

        Units: N/kg, which is the same as m/s². The field points toward the center of the mass.

        Field and force: the field belongs to the planet; the force belongs to the pair. Put a mass m in a field g and it feels Fg = mg. Both follow an inverse-square law: double the distance from the center and the field drops to ¼; triple it and it drops to ⅑.

        Gravitational field explorer

          8. Orbital speed and period

          A satellite in a circular orbit is in free fall. It falls toward Earth the whole time, but it moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath it, and it keeps missing. Gravity alone supplies the centripetal force.

          Worked example: a satellite in low orbit

          A satellite orbits 400 km above Earth’s surface, so r = 6.77 × 106 m from Earth’s center. Earth’s mass is 5.97 × 1024 kg. Find the satellite’s orbital speed and period.

          c (toward Earth)
          One lap

          ΣFc = mv²r

          Fg = mv²r

          GMmr² = mv²r

          v = √GMr

          v = √(6.67 × 10−11)(5.97 × 1024)6.77 × 106

          v = 7.67 × 103 m/s

          v = 2πrT

          T = 2πrv

          T = 2π(6.77 × 106)7.67 × 103v carried from the c-column

          T = 5.55 × 103 s ≈ 92 min

            Two results to remember:

            v = √GMr  T² = 4π²GMr³

            The second comes from putting v = 2πr/T into the first. Neither depends on the satellite’s mass. Farther orbits are slower and take longer.

            9. Kepler’s laws

            Johannes Kepler worked out three rules from years of careful observations of planets. Decades later, Newton showed that all three follow from his law of gravitation.

            1. Law of orbits

            Each planet moves in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. A circle is an ellipse with zero eccentricity; most planet orbits are close to circles.

            2. Law of areas

            The line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. A planet moves fastest when closest to the Sun (perihelion) and slowest when farthest (aphelion).

            3. Law of periods

            The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the orbit size, a (the semi-major axis). The ratio is the same for everything orbiting the same body.

            T² ∝ a³

            Kepler explorer

            Each shaded wedge takes the same amount of time. The wedges near the Sun are short and wide; the ones far away are long and thin. Their areas are equal.

            10. Common mistakes

            • Drawing “Fc” as an extra force. The centripetal force is the net force from real forces already on your diagram.
            • Drawing an outward “centrifugal” force. Nothing pushes you outward on a turn. You feel pressed outward because your body keeps trying to go straight (inertia) while the car turns under you.
            • Thinking a released object flies straight outward. It moves off along the tangent, in the direction of its velocity at that instant.
            • Using the string length as the radius of a conical pendulum. The radius is L sin θ.
            • Assuming FN = mg in a vertical circle. The normal force is bigger at the bottom of a dip and smaller at the top of a hill.
            • Saying astronauts float because there’s no gravity in space. At the ISS, g is about 8.7 m/s². Astronauts float because they and the station are falling together.
            • Measuring r from the surface. In Gm1m2/r², r is center to center: planet radius plus altitude.
            • Forgetting the square. Doubling the distance cuts gravity to one quarter, not one half.

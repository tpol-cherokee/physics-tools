1. Newton’s three laws

First law

If the net force is zero, velocity doesn’t change. The object stays at rest or keeps moving at a constant velocity.

ΣF = 0 ⟺ a = 0

Second law

A net force causes acceleration in the same direction as the net force. More mass means less acceleration.

ΣF = ma

Third law

Forces come in pairs: if A pushes on B, B pushes back on A just as hard. The two forces act on different objects.

FA on B = −FB on A

The big idea: net force tells you about acceleration, not velocity. A rock thrown straight up is moving up, but right after it leaves your hand the only force on it is gravity, pointing down. Its velocity is up; its acceleration is down.

2. The method

This is the same T-chart you used for equilibrium. The only change: the right side of each column is ma instead of 0. Equilibrium is just the special case where a = 0.

  1. Read for clue words. “At rest” or “constant velocity” means a = 0. “Speeds up,” “slows down,” or “accelerates” means a ≠ 0.
  2. Choose your object or system and draw a free-body diagram. Draw the acceleration as a separate arrow beside the diagram. ma is never a force.
  3. Choose axes so one points along the acceleration. On a ramp, tilt your axes to match the slope.
  4. Find all forces possible: Fg = mg, components of angled forces, and Ff = μFN.
  5. Draw the T-chart. Start with ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may. Write 0 on the right of any axis with no acceleration.
  6. Assign signs. Forces pointing the way the object accelerates are +; forces pointing the other way are −.
  7. Solve the column with only one unknown first, box the answer, and carry it across to the other column.
  8. Check: units, signs, and size. Is FN bigger or smaller than mg, and does that make sense?

    5. Elevators: when the y-column accelerates

    A bathroom scale doesn’t measure your weight. It measures how hard it pushes up on you: the normal force. In an elevator only the y-column matters, and its right side is ma. Take up as positive:

    x
    y

    nothing horizontal

    ΣFy = ma

    FN − Fg = ma

    FN = m(g + a)

    If the acceleration points up, a is positive and you feel heavier. If it points down, a is negative and you feel lighter. The direction you’re moving doesn’t matter, only the direction of the acceleration.

    6. Inclined planes

    On a ramp, tilt your axes. Let x run along the slope and y run perpendicular to it. Now FN and friction sit right on the axes, and only gravity has to be split into components.

    On a ramp: Fgx = mg sin θ (down the slope) and Fgy = mg cos θ (into the slope). With nothing else pushing into or pulling off the ramp, FN = mg cos θ, which is always less than mg.

    Worked example

    A 5 kg block slides down a 37° ramp. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.25. Find the block’s acceleration.

    y (perpendicular)

    Fgx − Ff = ma

    mg sin θ − Ff = ma

    Ff = μFN = 0.25(40) = 10 NFN carried from the y-column

    a = 50(0.6) − 105

    a = 4 m/s²

    ΣFy = 0

    FN − Fgy = 0

    FN = mg cos θ

    FN = 50(0.8)

      Special case: sliding at constant velocity

      If a block slides down a ramp at constant velocity, a = 0, so friction exactly balances Fgx:

      μ = FfFN = mg sin θmg cos θ = tan θ

      The mass cancels, so any object with this μ slides down at constant speed once it’s moving, heavy or light.

      7. Connected objects: treat them as a system

      When objects are tied, chained, or pushed together, they share one acceleration. Use two moves:

      1. Treat everything as one system to find the acceleration: ΣF = mTa. Tension between the objects is an internal force. It pulls one object forward and the other back by the same amount (Newton’s third law), so it cancels out of the system equation.
      2. Isolate one object to find the tension: ΣF = m1a. Pick the object with the fewest forces on it.

      For a string over a pulley, imagine pulling the string straight. Every force along the string either helps the motion (+) or opposes it (−).

        Worked example: ramp, pulley, and hanging block

        Block A (5 kg) sits on a 37° ramp with μ = 0.25. A string runs from A up over a pulley at the top of the ramp to block B (5 kg), which hangs straight down. Find the acceleration and the tension.

        First, find all forces possible

        Fg,A = mAg = 50 N and Fg,B = mBg = 50 N

        Fgx = 50 sin 37° = 30 N (down the ramp)

        FN = Fgy = 50 cos 37° = 40 N

        Ff = μFN = 0.25(40) = 10 N

        Whole system
        Block B alone

        Which way? Fg,B = 50 N beats Fgx = 30 N, so A slides up the ramp and friction on A points down the ramp.

        ΣF = mTa

        Fg,B − Fgx − Ff = (mA + mB)a

        a = 50 − 30 − 105 + 5

        a = 1 m/s²

        ΣF = mBa

        Fg,B − T = mBa

        T = 50 − 5(1)a carried from the system

        T = 45 N

          8. Deriving equations with symbols

          AP problems often say “derive an expression.” The method doesn’t change: you write the T-chart with letters instead of numbers, and you only use the symbols the problem gives you.

          A block of mass m is shoved up a ramp of angle θ. The coefficient of kinetic friction is μ. While the block slides up the ramp and slows down, derive an expression for the magnitude of its acceleration in terms of g, θ, and μ.

          x (along slope)
          y (perpendicular)

          ΣFx = ma

          Fgx + Ff = ma

          mg sin θ + μ(mg cos θ) = maFN carried from the y-column

          a = mg sin θ + μmg cos θm

          a = g(sin θ + μ cos θ)

          ΣFy = 0

          FN − Fgy = 0

          FN = mg cos θ

          Check: if θ = 0, a = μg (a flat floor). If μ = 0, a = g sin θ. ✓

            Habits for every derivation

            • Start from ΣF = ma (or ΣF = 0) and write each line as one step.
            • Use only the symbols given. If the problem gives m, θ, μ, and g, your answer can’t contain FN or Ff. Replace them.
            • Check limiting cases. Set an angle to 0 or a coefficient to 0 and see if the answer makes physical sense.

            9. Common mistakes

            • Drawing ma as a force on the free-body diagram. ma is the result of the forces. Draw the acceleration as a separate arrow beside the diagram.
            • Assuming force points the way the object moves. A rock thrown upward, a car braking, an elevator slowing on the way up: in each, the net force points opposite the motion.
            • Assuming FN = mg. It isn’t on a ramp, with an angled pull or push, or in an accelerating elevator. Always get FN from the y-column.
            • Mixing up sin and cos on a ramp. Down the slope is mg sin θ. Into the slope is mg cos θ. Quick check: at θ = 0 (flat), nothing should pull along the surface, and sin 0° = 0.
            • Setting tension equal to the hanging weight. That’s only true when nothing accelerates. A hanging block that speeds up downward has T < mg.
            • Putting internal forces in the system equation. Tension between two objects in your system cancels. Only outside forces go in ΣF = mTa.
